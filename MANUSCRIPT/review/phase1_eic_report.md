## EIC Review Report

### Reviewer Identity
Senior Associate Editor, *IEEE Transactions on Industry Applications* (Smart Grid / Data Center Energy Systems track), with secondary editorial experience at *IEEE Transactions on Sustainable Computing*. Specializes in DRL applications to power and computing systems; has handled several manuscripts extending or critiquing prior TIA publications.

### Overall Recommendation
Major Revision

### Confidence Score
4

### Summary Assessment
This manuscript reproduces a published TIA PPO-based multi-data-center migration scheduler [1], finds it statistically indistinguishable from a training-free greedy heuristic (§III-B, 0.08% gap), and proposes DFPS, a solver-distillation alternative that reaches within 3.2–3.8% of the strongest methods at ~3.6× less training compute than from-scratch RL (§VI-E, Table IV). The empirical discipline is genuinely strong: a frozen 600/100/100 train/validation/test split established before development, 5-seed statistics, Holm-corrected paired significance tests across 14 benchmarks (Table II), a mandatory ablation (Table III), robustness/OOD checks (Fig. 4–5), and a transparently reported mid-study bug and correction (§V-B). The paper is unusually candid about mixed results: a 7-win/7-loss record and an ablation showing two of three proposed architectural mechanisms are not load-bearing (§VI-C). However, as EIC I must weigh this rigor against two structural concerns: the contribution is explicitly scoped as narrow and derivative of a single prior TIA paper (§II "Positioning"), and the paper's actual center of gravity — training-compute efficiency and imitation-vs-RL methodology — is closer to a computing-systems study than a power-systems application, with all results confined to a synthetic scenario generator and no real market/grid data engagement. The abstract's claims are largely proportionate but omit that some of DFPS's "strongest cluster" losses (Table II) are against zero-training-cost heuristics, which materially affects the practical value proposition.

### Strengths
1. **Exceptional methodological discipline and transparency**: A pre-registered 600/100/100 scenario split "established before any candidate development and never altered based on results" (§V-A), 5-seed statistics with paired t-tests and Holm-Bonferroni correction across all 14 comparisons (§V-D, Table II), and a fully preserved chronology of a PID-Lagrangian saturation bug discovered and corrected *before* test-set use (§V-B, §IV-C) — this level of pre-registration discipline is rare in the DRL-for-scheduling literature and directly strengthens the credibility of the reported numbers.
2. **A genuinely novel reproduction-stage finding**: The demonstration that the fully-trained PPO reproduction of [1] is statistically indistinguishable from a zero-cost greedy heuristic (§III-B, $10,879.51 vs $10,870.51, 0.08% difference) is a substantive and citable finding in its own right, independent of DFPS, because it exposes a gap in the source paper's own evaluation protocol (no non-learned spatial baseline, §III-B).
3. **Honest ablation isolating the true source of the effect**: Table III and Fig. 5(a) show that removing the RL fine-tune or the topology encoder produces no measurable change (0.9 units on a $196 std.), while removing distillation costs 5.6% and triples seed variance — the authors explicitly report this as "honest, if humbling" (§VI-C) rather than retrofitting a narrative around the architecture they built.
4. **Quantified, non-cherry-picked efficiency claim**: The 3.6× total-compute reduction (Table IV, Fig. 6: ~55 min vs ~200 min across 5 seeds) is grounded in a concrete, itemized accounting (one-time 51-minute CP-SAT teacher cost amortized across seeds, §VI-E) rather than an unsubstantiated headline number.

### Weaknesses
1. **The practical value proposition is undercut by losses to zero-cost heuristics**: Table II shows DFPS loses to HEFT (-3.28%) and Greedy spatiotemporal (-3.32%) — both training-free, requiring no compute at all — yet the abstract and §VI-E frame the efficiency contribution ("3.6× less total training compute") only against RL alternatives. Since §III-B's own motivating finding is that even from-scratch PPO cannot beat a free heuristic, the paper needs to directly confront why a multi-stage learned pipeline (CP-SAT teacher + imitation + RL fine-tune, Fig. 1–2) is preferable to simply deploying HEFT or the greedy heuristic in production. *Suggested fix*: add an explicit discussion (e.g., adaptivity to distribution shift, decision latency under load, or scenarios where heuristics would be expected to degrade) that gives DFPS a value proposition beyond "cheaper than RL."
2. **Thin power-systems/grid engagement relative to TIA's application scope**: All experiments use a "frozen synthetic scenario generator" (§III-A, V-A) that qualitatively matches [1]'s described price/CEF patterns but uses no real electricity market or grid carbon-intensity data; the underlying market/grid model is inherited unmodified from [1] and is not itself scrutinized or varied. For a Smart Grid/DC Energy Systems submission, I would expect at least a sensitivity analysis tied to a real or semi-real ISO/CEF dataset, or an explicit discussion of what changes under realistic market-clearing dynamics. As written, the paper's substantive content (imitation learning vs. on-policy RL, training-compute accounting, Table IV parameter counts) reads as an ML-methodology study wearing an energy-systems application. *Suggested fix*: incorporate at least one real or semi-real price/CEF trace, or add a dedicated subsection translating findings into grid-operator/utility implications.
3. **Contribution is explicitly self-scoped as a critique/extension of a single prior TIA paper**: §II "Positioning" disclaims novelty in carbon-aware scheduling, topology-aware scheduling, and PPO-for-DC-scheduling individually, and Section III is devoted almost entirely to reproducing and auditing [1]. This is legitimate and well-precedented at TIA, but combined with weakness #2, the manuscript risks reading as an extended technical comment on [1] rather than an independent contribution. *Suggested fix*: strengthen the Introduction/Conclusion with a standalone forward-looking claim about solver-distillation's applicability beyond this one reproduced problem instance (e.g., preliminary evidence it would transfer to a different DC-scheduling formulation).
4. **Abstract omits a material nuance about the "strongest cluster"**: The abstract states DFPS is "practically competitive (within 0.3–3.8%) with the strongest cluster" without disclosing that this cluster includes two zero-training-cost heuristics (HEFT, Greedy spatiotemporal — visible only in Table II, p.10). Given the paper's otherwise exemplary candor (7-win/7-loss is reported plainly), this specific omission is likely inadvertent rather than deliberate, but it is the one place where the abstract's proportionality falls short of the body's honesty. *Suggested fix*: a single clause, e.g. "...including two training-free heuristics," would close the gap and would if anything *strengthen* the paper's credibility.
5. **Generalizability is bounded to one synthetic problem family**: Robustness checks (Fig. 4: workload counts 6–9, deadline-slack 0.5–1.5×; Fig. 5(b): sensor noise, bandwidth dropout) are valuable but remain within the same generator, DC count (3), and market design used throughout. The "for this problem class" language in the Abstract and §I is broader than what is empirically demonstrated. *Suggested fix*: soften scope claims or add at least one structurally different scenario (e.g., 5+ DCs, alternative market-clearing rule) to support generalization language.

### Detailed Comments

#### Journal Fit
Mixed. The topic — DRL scheduling for multi-DC workloads under electricity/carbon markets — is squarely within TIA's Smart Grid/DC Energy Systems scope, and there is direct precedent since the manuscript extends/critiques a paper TIA itself published [1]. However, the manuscript's actual technical center of mass (imitation learning from a MILP teacher, training-compute accounting in minutes, parameter counts, decision-latency in milliseconds — Table IV) is generic ML/scheduling-systems content that would fit equally or better in a computing-efficiency venue (e.g., IEEE Transactions on Sustainable Computing, per my secondary editorial experience). The complete absence of real market/grid data (weakness #2) is the crux of my hesitation on fit: TIA readers will expect stronger power-systems grounding than a fully synthetic scenario generator provides. I would not reject on fit grounds alone given the direct lineage to [1], but I would ask the authors to either strengthen the power-systems grounding or make an explicit case for TIA relevance in the Introduction.

#### Originality
The paper is candid and correctly self-aware about the boundaries of its novelty (§II "Positioning": no claim to carbon-awareness, topology-awareness, or PPO-for-DC-scheduling individually). Its narrow, defensible originality claim is the application of full-solution solver distillation (as opposed to solver-internal branching-policy distillation, cf. [9]–[11]) to this specific dependency-aware, multi-market DC scheduling problem, compared head-to-head against from-scratch RL trained to the same budget (§II "Learning for combinatorial optimization"). This is a legitimate, if modest, methodological contribution. The reproduction-stage finding (§III-B) is arguably the paper's most original single result and deserves more prominence than its current placement as a motivating aside.

#### Significance
Moderate and field-local rather than broad. If the findings hold, they matter primarily to a specific niche: researchers benchmarking DRL schedulers for multi-market DC workload migration. The significance is tempered by weakness #1 — the proposed method (DFPS) itself loses to free heuristics, which somewhat blunts the "is expensive RL training necessary" question posed in §I, since the deeper implied answer ("maybe no learned method is necessary at all for this instance family") is not fully confronted. The efficiency argument (3.6×) is real but narrower than the abstract implies once heuristic baselines are factored in.

#### Structural Coherence
Generally strong: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Conclusion track consistently, and the Conclusion (§VIII) faithfully restates the paper's actual findings without inflating them. One coherence gap: the central research question in §I ("is expensive on-policy RL training necessary at all... or can a cheaper alternative reach comparable quality?") is answered obliquely — the Conclusion emphasizes DFPS's efficiency relative to RL but does not explicitly revisit that HEFT/Greedy already answer "no" to the RQ at zero cost, which is arguably the more radical implication of the paper's own data (Table II).

#### Title & Abstract
The title is accurate and appropriately scoped ("Solver-Distilled Reinforcement Learning for Multi-Data-Center Migration Scheduling"). The abstract is unusually candid for the genre — it leads with a negative reproduction finding and reports the 7-win/7-loss record plainly rather than only the wins. My one concrete calibration concern (weakness #4) is the missing disclosure that some "strongest cluster" losses are against zero-cost heuristics; this is a proportionality issue worth fixing but does not amount to over-claiming in the aggregate — if anything the abstract under-sells how mixed the practical picture is.

#### Conclusion
The conclusion (§VIII) accurately reflects the paper's body: it restates the reproduction finding, the DFPS efficiency result, the ablation's humbling implication (training methodology, not architecture, drives the gain), and the unresolved carbon-compliance trade-off (§VI-F) without concealment. This is a well-earned, honest conclusion. It would be strengthened by directly addressing the free-heuristic comparison and by articulating a clearer standalone (non-[1]-dependent) forward-looking claim, per weaknesses #1 and #3.

### Questions for Authors
1. Given that HEFT and Greedy spatiotemporal (Table I, Table II) require zero training cost yet outperform DFPS by 3.28–3.32%, what is the practical deployment case for DFPS over simply using these free heuristics in production?
2. Would the solver-distillation methodology and the ablation's conclusion (training methodology > architecture) be expected to generalize to different DC counts, market designs, or real (non-synthetic) electricity/CEF data? Is a partially real dataset feasible for a revision?
3. Do the authors view this manuscript as a standalone contribution to DC scheduling methodology, or primarily as a technical comment/extension on [1]? Please make this framing decision explicit, as it affects how broadly the Introduction's claims should be read by readers unfamiliar with [1].
4. Why is IEEE TIA the intended target venue rather than a computing-systems/ML-efficiency venue, given that the paper's central technical contribution (imitation-learning vs. on-policy RL training cost) is not power-systems-specific?
5. In Table IV, CP-SAT's reported cost (1,750.0 min, 17,500.00 ms latency) reflects the *teacher-generation* cost — please clarify in the table caption that this is not a deployment-time comparison, to avoid readers misreading CP-SAT as impractically slow at inference relative to the learned methods.

### Minor Issues
- Abstract: add a clause disclosing that some benchmarks in the "strongest cluster" (Table II: HEFT, Greedy spatiotemporal) are training-free, to fully close the proportionality gap noted under Weakness #4.
- Table I / Table IV: latency is shown as "–" for heuristics in Table I but as a large solve-time figure for CP-SAT in Table IV (17,500 ms); a cross-referencing footnote would prevent reader confusion about what "latency" means for non-learned methods.
- Fig. 6: HEFT and Greedy (DFPS's actual losses, per Table II) are not plotted since they have no training cost; consider annotating their cost position near the x-axis origin or noting their omission explicitly in the caption, since they are directly relevant to the Pareto argument the figure is making.
- References [2]–[11] are listed without author names (generic titles only); ensure the camera-ready reference list is complete.
- The "Anonymous Authors" byline and blinding footnote are appropriate for a review copy; please confirm with the editorial office whether IEEE TIA's standard submission process requires author identification at this stage, and ensure a compliant non-blinded version is ready if required.

### Recommendation to Peer Reviewers
I recommend the methodology reviewer scrutinize: (a) the sufficiency of the CP-SAT teacher-label set (995 samples from 199 scenarios, §IV-D) for reliable imitation-learning generalization, and (b) whether 5-seed variance estimates and Holm correction across 14 simultaneous comparisons (Table II) are statistically well-powered given the effect sizes reported. I recommend the domain/power-systems reviewer assess whether the fully synthetic scenario generator (§III-A) is an adequate proxy for real electricity/carbon market dynamics, and independently verify the fairness and completeness of the reproduction of [1] (the authors note no author code or data was available). I recommend the perspective and devil's-advocate reviewers stress-test the question I raise most emphatically here: whether a proposed method that loses to two zero-cost heuristics (Table II) retains sufficient practical significance to justify the paper's framing as a positive methodological contribution, versus being more honestly framed as a second negative result reinforcing the paper's own reproduction-stage finding.
